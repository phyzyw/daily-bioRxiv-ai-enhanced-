Abstract: Sleep plays a critical role in stabilising memory. However, whether the architecture of sleep co-evolves with investment in learning and memory is unknown. To address this, we exploit the extreme levels of neural and behavioural divergence among Heliconiini butterflies. Compared to other Heliconiini, Heliconius have massively expanded mushroom bodies, the insect centres of learning and memory, which are associated with specific enhancements in visual learning and memory. Using a purpose-built video system, machine learning-facilitated behavioural tracking, and in-depth analysis of micro-behaviours during nighttime quiescence, we describe multi-state sleep in Heliconius melpomene and an outgroup Heliconiini, Dryas iulia. In both species, sleep is predominantly composed of quiet sleep, however, Heliconius and Dryas deviate in specific features. In Dryas iulia, quiet sleep is characterised by highly stereotyped, rhythmic antennal oscillations. Conversely, the antennae of Heliconius melpomene remain largely motionless but occasionally perform large movements which interrupt quiet sleep. These antennal deflections are associated with bouts of activity that include increased overall antennal displacement, isolated head twitching, proboscis movements and less frequently, wing movements. While these episodes were also seen in Dryas iulia, they were considerably less frequent. We further demonstrate that the behavioural profile of these active sleep bouts is constituently different from bouts of induced wakefulness, providing experimental support for multi-state sleep in Lepidoptera. This work shows that Heliconius melpomene, a species distinguished by its expanded mushroom bodies, exhibits more frequent bouts of active sleep than the outgroup species Dryas iulia. These findings raise the possibility that increased investment in learning and memory, as well as the structures that support it, may be associated with changes in sleep architecture.

Abstract: Emerging Plasmodium falciparum resistance to frontline artemisinin (ART) derivatives threatens global malaria control efforts. Current protocols assigning ART-sensitive (ART-S) or -resistant (ART-R) phenotypes to parasite strains with Kelch13 (K13) mutations remain time-intensive for real-time surveillance. Ring-survival assay (RSA), an ex vivo gold standard mandates laboratory-adaptation under culture conditions and frequently does not replicate clinical ART-responsiveness, thereby limiting its utility for rapidly triaging newly emerging K13 variants. In contrast, advent of artificial intelligence (AI)-derived models hold potential to screen >100 K13 variants within hours, enabling rapid near-real-time prediction of ART-S/R. Here, we engineered a panel of forty representative K13 variants by computing their relative deviations from wild-type K13 across ten physicochemical and structural descriptors, yielding a compact feature set which captures mutation-induced perturbations in folding, stability, and solvent exposure. These can be further utilized to train and validate AI classifier to predict resistance.

Abstract: We introduce Cadense, a generative framework for antibody ensemble prediction that produces diverse Fv structures from sequence, informed by conformational dynamics. While a valuable resource, static structure prediction provides only a single snapshot of dynamic proteins, whose motions influence their binding and other properties. This is particularly relevant for antibodies, an important class of molecules for drug discovery, where flexible complementarity determining region (CDR) loops are difficult to model with static approaches. Cadense addresses this gap by generating ensembles that match conformational and biophysical property distributions from molecular dynamics (MD), while reducing the computational burden from days of simulation to seconds of generation. We show Cadense ensembles can recover both apo and holo conformations of the same antibody and serve as effective input to physics-based docking, capturing the conformational flexibility important to properly model antibody antigen interactions. Together, these results establish Cadense as a useful tool for antibody conformational ensemble generation with the promise to enable faster, dynamics-aware antibody design.

Abstract: RNA self-association is a key elementary event behind many cellular processes from ribosome assembly, to regulation of gene expression, to assembly of biomolecular condensates. While RNAs self-interact in many ways, one direct and unambiguous sequence-encoded pattern for RNA self-recognition is a palindrome, or palindromic motif. In isolation palindromic motifs tend to fold back on themselves, favoring stable stem-loop secondary structures. However, palindromes have been implicated in some cases of RNA self-recognition, segregation and phase separation, raising the question of which palindromes in a given RNA are actually available for intermolecular engagement. Toward answering this question, we define three classes of palindromic regions based on local secondary-structure context, and degree of availability for interactions via their nucleobases, sorting into three classes. We then apply this framework across known structures of RNA chains and find that the majority of palindromic regions are bound up in secondary structures. We then designed a minimal set of short stem-loop RNA sequences to evaluate the self-interaction stability of each defined palindrome class using coarse-grained molecular dynamics simulations. Overall, this work provides a residue-level, structure-aware framework that connects RNA sequence, secondary-structure context, and intermolecular self-association for two interacting RNA chains.
