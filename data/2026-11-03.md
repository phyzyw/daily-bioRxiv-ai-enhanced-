Abstract: Conventional low-pass filters attenuate or remove high-frequency Fourier amplitudes but leave the phases intact wherever the amplitude response stays above zero. As a result, fine-scale detail of the original signal can persist beyond the target resolution. A sharp cutoff removes this detail but produces ringing artifacts. To address both limitations, this work introduces ramped phase randomization, which extends a conventional low-pass filter by adding random offsets to Fourier phases that increase smoothly with spatial frequency. For cryo-EM maps, the correlation between a map at the target resolution and an ideal, noiseless map sets the levels of the phase offsets, with a correction for the finite resolution of the input map. On three published map pairs, ramped phase filtering reduced the higher resolution parent's fine detail to a level similar to that of independently determined maps at the lower target resolution, whereas amplitude-only filtering kept the parent's sharp features. The FSCs between the filtered maps and their parents closely resembled those of the experimental maps. The same filter also serves as a data augmentation approach for neural network-based machine learning. With ramped phase randomization, each augmented copy keeps the power spectrum and low-frequency content of the original example but has a different high-frequency pattern. For simulated CryoBench particles of 16 ribosome assembly states, phase augmentation raised mean test accuracy in all 20 combinations of training-set size and latent dimension, by 20.8 percentage points on average (range from 3.3 points to 44.2 points). On two general deep learning benchmarks, mean test accuracy rose from 92.15% to 96.99% with 60 labelled UCI Synthetic Control traces and from 53.44% to 74.27% with 500 labelled noisy MNIST images. The ramped phase randomization is implemented in an open-source Python package, with a companion browser application for interactive visualization of cryo-EM maps and filtering effects.

Abstract: Regulatory DNA controls when and where genes are expressed, making it a key driver of phenotypic evolution. Yet detecting selection in non-coding regions remains difficult, as most approaches rely on sequence conservation or changes in substitution rate rather than molecular effects. RegEvol bridges this gap by linking machine learning-based predictions of transcription factor binding to explicit evolutionary models. It uses the distribution of predicted mutational effects to infer fitness functions under different evolutionary scenarios including random drift, stabilising selection, and directional selection. Through maximum-likelihood estimation, it identifies the regime that best explains observed changes along a lineage from an ancestral sequence. When substitution numbers are limited, such as along short evolutionary branches, likelihood differences can be aggregated across sets of regulatory elements to increase statistical power. RegEvol corrects biases that affected previous tests based on machine learning of transcription factor binding, while remaining conservative across different levels of divergence. Applied to over 3 million Drosophila melanogaster regulatory regions, we identify 5.1% under directional selection, enriched near reproductive and immune genes. Applying the aggregation strategy to human CTCF binding across tissues reveals enrichment of directional signals in nervous and male reproductive systems. The framework is readily applicable to experimentally detected regulatory elements with alignable ancestral sequences and is flexible to future advances in understanding regulatory function, providing a powerful basis for investigating adaptation in non-coding regions.

Abstract: Receptor flexibility complicates molecular docking when only an unbound (apo) protein structure is available. Local side-chain rearrangements can alter binding-pocket accessibility and ligand complementarity, motivating approaches that sample these motions efficiently. Here, we evaluate a targeted Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) protocol that combines local and larger side-chain torsional proposals with periodic molecular dynamics (MD) relaxation under harmonic backbone restraints. Receptor ensembles generated from an apo structure were evaluated against 859 experimental complexes from the OpenBind 2A protease benchmark and compared with the apo receptor and ensembles generated by restrained and unrestrained MD. With regular-space representative selection, MCMC recovered a ligand pose within 2 [A] of the experimental pose for 74.4% of complexes, compared with 51.0% for apo and 59.5% for restrained MD. Median minimum ligand heavy-atom RMSD decreased from 1.96 [A] for apo and 1.82 [A] for restrained MD to 1.62 [A] for MCMC. The largest gains occurred for complexes with greater side-chain displacement relative to pocket backbone displacement, consistent with the motions targeted by the protocol. MCMC representatives retained pocket geometries within the experimentally observed range, whereas unrestrained-MD representatives exhibited reduced pocket free volume and poor recovery. These findings support targeted side-chain sampling as a practical approach to generating apo-derived docking ensembles. The reported improvements measure best-case pose coverage across generated poses and receptors; their extension to prospective pose ranking and virtual screening remains to be established.

Abstract: Triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 (TREM2) is a regulator of microglial function in Alzheimer's disease (AD), and its soluble ectodomain (sTREM2) has emerged as a potential neuroprotective factor. Taken together, our results define a mechanistic framework for the sTREM2-TG2 axis in AD and establish a structure-guided strategy for developing peptide modulators of this interaction.
